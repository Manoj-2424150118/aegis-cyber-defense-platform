What are DMARC, SPF and DKIM?

Email was designed without sender identity, and these three DNS records are the retrofit. Together they decide whether a stranger can send mail that claims to be from your domain — the trick behind most CEO fraud.

SPF: who may send

A Sender Policy Framework record lists the servers allowed to send for a domain. Receivers compare the sending server's IP against the list. Two limits matter: SPF authenticates the envelope sender (often invisible to you), and it breaks after ten DNS lookups — a limit bloated records silently exceed, voiding the check.

DKIM: a signature on the message

DomainKeys Identified Mail signs message headers and body with a private key; receivers verify with the public key published in DNS under a selector (selector._domainkey.domain). A valid signature proves the mail really passed through the domain's infrastructure unmodified. Keys under 2048 bits are below the modern baseline.

DMARC: the policy that ties them together

DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance) requires that SPF or DKIM align with the domain in the visible From header — the one humans read — and tells receivers what to do on failure: p=none (watch only), p=quarantine (spam folder), or p=reject (refuse delivery). Only enforcement policies actually stop spoofing; p=none forever is a reporting project, not protection. Watch also for pct below 100 (partial rollout) and a weak subdomain policy (sp=), which leave subdomains spoofable.

Why it matters to you

  • Receiving: a sender domain with enforced DMARC is measurably harder to impersonate. Check the domains that email you about money with the domain security checker.
  • Sending: without DMARC enforcement, anyone can email your customers, staff or family as you — and you cannot stop them.
  • Investigating: in a suspicious email's headers, failed or misaligned authentication plus a payment request is the classic business-email-compromise signature. The email analyser reads those results for you.

A sane rollout order

Inventory your senders → publish SPF and DKIM for each → DMARC at p=none with reporting to learn your traffic → fix stragglers → raise to quarantine, then reject, with pct=100 and an explicit subdomain policy. Most small domains finish in a week; the reporting stage is where the surprises live.
