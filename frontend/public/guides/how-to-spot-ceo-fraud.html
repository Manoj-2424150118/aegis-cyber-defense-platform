How to spot CEO fraud (business email compromise)

CEO fraud needs no malware and no broken password. It needs one convincing email, one busy employee, and one wire transfer. It is, by reported losses, the most expensive category of cybercrime — and it is stopped by habits, not software.

The playbook

  • The setup: "I'm in a meeting / travelling — handle this quietly." The impersonated executive is always unavailable for a call, by construction.
  • The ask: an urgent wire, a supplier's "new bank details", gift cards for clients, or payroll sent to a changed account. The specific ask varies; the structure does not.
  • The pressure: confidentiality plus deadline. Secrecy isolates the victim from the one colleague who would spot it; urgency disables deliberation.
  • The address: either a look-alike domain one letter off, a free-mail account with the executive's name, or — worst case — the real mailbox of a compromised supplier. Authentication checks help with the first two and say nothing about the third.

Five verification habits that defeat it

  • Call a known number. Any payment instruction or bank-detail change gets confirmed by voice on a number from your records — never one in the email.
  • Two-person rule. Payments above a threshold need a second approver who did not receive the request.
  • Slow is a control. A same-day exception process is the attack surface. Legitimate urgencies survive a one-hour verification delay.
  • Read the headers. Reply-To pointing somewhere unexpected, authentication failures, a first-ever thread with no history — paste the message into the email analyser for a structured read.
  • Check the domain. A look-alike sending domain registered days ago shows up in the domain checker via RDAP age and missing DMARC.

If the money already moved

Call the bank immediately — wire recalls are a race measured in hours, and the first day decides most recoveries. Then preserve the emails with full headers, report to your national cybercrime portal (India: cybercrime.gov.in; US: ic3.gov), and notify your insurer if you have one. Silence helps only the fraudster; fast reporting also protects the next target.
